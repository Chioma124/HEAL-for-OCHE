Your Child's Rights and the IEP Process

Understand your child's rights and prepare for an IEP meeting.

Your Child's Rights and the IEP Process

Federal law guarantees children with disabilities, including many medical conditions, access to special education services and a supportive learning environment. These guides explain what that means and how to prepare for an IEP meeting.

  • Special Education and Your Rights
    A guide to getting special education services, contesting an evaluation, making sure an IEP is followed, and what to do if your child is suspended, expelled, or removed from school.
  • Procedural Safeguards (2022)
    An overview of the educational rights and procedural safeguards guaranteed to students with disabilities and their guardians under IDEA. Schools must provide this to you at least once per year.
  • 18 Tips for Your IEP Meeting
    How to approach an IEP meeting, conduct yourself in it, and follow up afterward.
  • IEP Advocacy Tips (Disability Rights California)
    Tips for guardians before, during, and after an IEP meeting, including how to push back when services are denied and how to document communication with the school district.
  • IEP and Me: Video Series
    A video series that walks parents and providers through the IEP process, from eligibility and goal-setting to placement and family rights.
  • Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE)
    If you disagree with the school's evaluation of your child, you have the right to request an Independent Educational Evaluation conducted by a qualified professional outside the school district at no cost to you.
  • Types of Special Education and How to Apply
    Step-by-step guide explaining special education, how to request an evaluation, and what an IEP covers. Includes key timelines and what to do if the school says no.
  • What is a 504 Plan?
    If your child does not qualify for special education under IDEA, they may qualify for a 504 plan under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act — which allows accommodations like extra time on tests or testing in a quiet room.
  • IEP Tip Sheet
    A quick reference guide with practical tips for families preparing for and attending IEP meetings. Includes key questions to ask and what to look for in your child's IEP goals.

Getting an Evaluation Started

Request a school evaluation with a letter you can fill in and send.

Getting an Evaluation Started

The first step toward an IEP or 504 Plan is usually a written request for an evaluation. You can write this yourself, or ask your child's doctor to send a letter on your behalf.

  • Special Education Assessment Request Letter (for guardians)
    A letter template you can fill in and send to your child's school to request an evaluation.
  • Sample IEP Request Letter (English)
    A model letter requesting a referral for a special education evaluation, with a sample and a fillable version.
  • Sample IEP Request Letter (Spanish)
    Carta modelo para solicitar una evaluación de educación especial, con una versión de muestra y una versión rellenable.
  • IEP Sample Letter (English)
    Sample letter for IEP-related communication with school districts.
  • IEP Sample Letter (Spanish)
    Carta de muestra para comunicacion relacionada con el IEP con los distritos escolares.
  • California Special Education Guide Letter
    A guide and model letter for requesting a referral for evaluation, including how to reference your rights under California Education Code.
  • IEP Meeting Request Letter
    Template for formally requesting an IEP meeting with your school district. You have the right to request a meeting at any time — you do not have to wait for the annual review.
  • Letter Requesting Evaluation Results
    Template for requesting copies of your child's assessment results and reports from the school district. You have the right to receive these in a language you understand.

For Schools & Educators

Information, advice and resources for teachers, counselors and school staff

When a student returns to your classroom after a hospitalization, or is managing a chronic condition alongside their schoolwork, a little context goes a long way. These resources are written for teachers, counselors, and school staff supporting that student.

On this page: Welcoming a Student Back After Hospitalization · Classroom Accommodations · Attendance, Home, and Hospital Instruction Policies

Welcoming a Student Back After Hospitalization

Students coming back after a hospital stay often need some flexibility while they regain their footing academically and socially. Before the student returns, it helps to know what their medical team and family expect for the coming weeks, and to have a plan for missed work that is realistic rather than overwhelming. The Medical Conditions & School section has condition-specific information you can share with your team.

Get More Support

When you need more than a document

This website is the first step: information and documents you can use on your own. Some families need more hands-on help, and that's available too.

On this page: Parent Mentor Program (PMP) · Support Teams · Local Resources

Parent Mentor Program (PMP)

We recommend starting with Parent Mentors (FamilyCenteredCare@stanfordchildrens.org).

Parent mentors are experienced parents trained to support families whose children are receiving care at Lucile Packard Children’s Hospital Stanford. They offer encouragement and practical suggestions for navigating hospital stays, communicating with the healthcare team, preparing for discharge, and managing care at home. Families can connect with mentors at the bedside, during clinic visits, or by phone, with visits coordinated and supervised by hospital social workers.

Support Teams

We also work with some other teams who may help to support your family's needs.

  • Hospital Education Advocacy Liaisons (HEAL)
    Educational specialists within the hospital. They help translate your child's medical and learning needs into practical school supports, and can attend IEP meetings with you. Call (650) 725-2381 and leave a message (response within 7 to 10 days), or email HEAL@stanfordchildrens.org.
  • Family Empowerment Centers
    Community organizations that help families connect with special education services, regional centers, public benefits, and other community resources. Support ranges from a single phone consultation to one-on-one navigation for families with more complex needs. Find your county's center with the finder tool.

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of terms you may hear at school and in the hospital

The glossary is currently available in English only.

School and hospital teams use a lot of shortened terms. Look up a term here, or hover over a dotted underline in the resource descriptions to see its meaning.

For more special education terms, see the Disability Rights California Glossary.

ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ
No matching terms. Try another word.

#

504 Plan
A plan under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act that gives a student with a disability accommodations, such as extra time on tests or testing in a quiet room, so they can learn alongside classmates. A student can qualify for a 504 plan even if they do not qualify for special education.
See related resources

A

ABA (Applied Behavior Analysis)
A structured teaching approach used to build skills and reduce challenging behaviors. Schools may provide it as a related service if a child needs it to benefit from their education.
Accommodation
A change in how a student learns or is tested, such as extra time, a quiet room, or a copy of class notes. An accommodation does not change what the student is expected to learn.
See related resources
Annual review
A meeting held at least once a year where the IEP team reviews a student's progress and updates the IEP. You do not have to wait for the annual review to ask for an IEP meeting.
See related resources
Assessment (evaluation)
The tests, observations, and interviews a school does to decide whether a child qualifies for special education and what the child needs. You can ask for one in writing. The school must respond within 15 days and complete the assessment within 60 days.
See related resources

C

CSHCN (Children with Special Health Care Needs)
Children who have, or are at higher risk for, a chronic physical, developmental, behavioral, or emotional condition and who need health and related services beyond what children generally need.

E

Educational placement
The setting where a student receives their education, such as a general classroom, a special day class, home hospital instruction, or independent study.
See related resources

F

FAPE (Free Appropriate Public Education)
The right of every eligible student with a disability to a public education at no cost to the family that is designed to meet the student's individual needs. This right comes from IDEA.

H

HEAL (Hospital Education Advocacy Liaisons)
A free program at Stanford Medicine Children's Health that helps children ages 3 to 18 succeed in school after a diagnosis of a medical condition or disability.
See related resources
Home hospital instruction
Instruction provided at home or in the hospital for a student who is temporarily unable to attend school because of a medical condition.
See related resources

I

IDEA (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act)
The federal law that guarantees eligible children with disabilities a free appropriate public education in the least restrictive environment, and that sets out the rights of students and families.
See related resources
IEE (Independent Educational Evaluation)
If you disagree with the school's evaluation of your child, you can ask for an evaluation by a qualified professional outside the school district, at no cost to you.
See related resources
IEP (Individualized Education Plan)
A legally binding document that describes a student's educational needs, goals, and the services the school will provide. Children from preschool through 12th grade may qualify.
See related resources
Independent study
A learning option where a student completes schoolwork mostly at home with a teacher's guidance and regular check-ins. It can help students who cannot attend school in person during treatment.
See related resources

L

Least restrictive environment (LRE)
The principle that students with disabilities should learn alongside students without disabilities as much as is appropriate for their needs.

M

Modification
A change in what a student is expected to learn or be tested on, such as shorter or simpler assignments. This is different from an accommodation, which changes how a student learns.

O

OT (Occupational Therapy)
Therapy that helps with everyday school tasks such as handwriting, using classroom tools, and caring for oneself. Schools can provide it as a related service.

P

Parent mentor
A trained parent with experience caring for a child with medical needs who offers support to other parents. Stanford Medicine Children's Health has a Parent Mentor Program.

About Us

A partnership to keep a child's health from standing in the way of their education

This resource hub is a partnership between the Office of Child Health Equity's Health and Education Access Hub at Stanford Medicine, Hospital Education Advocacy Liaisons (HEAL), and Parents Helping Parents (PHP). Our goal is to make sure a child's health, language, or family circumstances never stand in the way of their education.

Questions, corrections, or a resource you'd like to see added? Contact us at officeofchildhealthequity@stanford.edu.

Our mission

The Health and Education Access Hub aims to address the persistent barriers Children with Special Health Care Needs face in accessing appropriate education. Our long-term goal is to prevent any child's education from being compromised due to health and to promote equity for children and families navigating both the healthcare and education systems.

Why this matters

Every year, thousands of children with serious health conditions spend extended periods of time in the hospital. Although laws protect the educational rights of these children, many families face significant challenges when their child returns to school. Communication between hospitals and schools is often poor, resources are limited, and families struggle to navigate a complicated system.

About the HEAL program

Hospital Education Advocacy Liaisons (HEAL) helps children succeed in school after they are diagnosed with a medical condition or disability. It is a free program that works with you to find support that meets your child's learning and behavioral needs.

HEAL serves children who:

  • Are patients of Stanford Medicine Children's Health
  • Are 3 to 18 years old
  • Have medical conditions or disabilities that affect learning
